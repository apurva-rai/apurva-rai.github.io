Ph.D. candidate in Computer Science at the University of California, Irvine, advised by Stanislaw Jarecki.

Before two people can talk securely, each needs to know who is on the other end. Encryption alone cannot tell them.

Today they usually rely on a trusted third party, such as a certificate authority or a messaging app’s key server. I work on protocols that need less trust in such a party, or none. In these protocols, people check each other by comparing a short code, or they log in with a password or their own biometric data. This work has appeared at ASIACRYPT 2025 and SCN 2026 and will appear at CANS 2026.

I expect to finish my Ph.D. in June 2027 and am looking for positions that start in fall 2027.

Research

My coauthors and I give each protocol a formal security definition and a proof, and we design it to fit into protocols people already run. Three papers are published or accepted. Two more are in preparation, both with implementations and measurements. I write most of my code in C, Rust and Python.

Comparing a short code

Signal and WhatsApp hand out each user’s public keys from a server, and a compromised server can hand out the wrong ones. My coauthors and I designed a check that lets two people confirm each other’s keys by comparing six digits. It fits Signal’s existing handshake, adds one message, needs no change to the key server and stays secure even if that server is compromised (ASIACRYPT 2025).

Deniability and group chats

Signal’s handshake is deniable. A saved transcript cannot prove to anyone else that two people talked. Our first design lost that property, and in follow-up work we restored it in every case but one (SCN 2026). My advisor and I then extended the check to groups, including the protocol behind group chats in Signal and WhatsApp, with no extra round of messages (CANS 2026, to appear).

Logging in with a face

A leaked biometric cannot be replaced like a password, and readings are noisy, so a protocol has to accept a close but inexact match. In a manuscript in preparation, my coauthors and I give a protocol for remote biometric login in which the server stores only a one-way hash of each person’s biometric data and never the data itself. After a breach, an attacker can do no better than guess faces one at a time. We also give a formula that predicts how often the wrong face is accepted, including at settings too strict for any experiment to check. We implemented the protocol and measured its speed and accuracy.

Keys from a password, hidden in ordinary traffic

Some authorities mandate encryption they can read and can later compel people to hand over their secrets. A paper in preparation with Stanislaw Jarecki and Jinyao Xu shows how two people who share only a password can agree on a hidden key inside that traffic. Their recorded sessions still look innocent after they are forced to reveal the password. We prove that this property needs three messages, and we give three protocols that use only three. One is built on ML-KEM, the standard designed to resist quantum computers. A session of either of the other two costs each party under 0.2 milliseconds in our prototype.

Assumptions behind security proofs

A security proof holds only under its assumptions. My working paper, What a Security Proof Cannot Prove, sorts those assumptions by what each one depends on, such as a party’s honesty or a secret staying secret, and by who must uphold it. I applied the classification to the web’s certificate system, to Signal and to synced passkeys.

What comes next

  • Biometric login against generative attackers. Published attacks build synthetic faces that match many people at once. I plan to measure those attacks at the strict thresholds our protocol uses.
  • Post-quantum short-code authentication. Our short-code protocols rest on elliptic curves, which a quantum computer could break. People trust a verified key for years, and such a computer may exist within that time. My goal is a post-quantum version that still adds only one message and keeps deniability.
  • A security bound that includes the person. Six digits protect people only if they notice a mismatch. In one study of codes read aloud over a call, listeners accepted 30 to 40 percent of mismatched word codes. I plan to define security in a way that counts how often people miss a mismatch.

Papers

The three conference papers list authors alphabetically, the convention in cryptography. I was a lead contributor to each.

Conference papers

  • Security without Trusted Third Parties: VRF-Based Authentication with Short Authenticated Strings Y. Gu, S. Jarecki, P. Nazarian, A. Rai. ASIACRYPT 2025. ePrintDOI
    Summary

    Earlier short-code protocols need three rounds of messages in every session. Ours is built from a verifiable random function, a keyed function whose outputs anyone can check against a public key. Its first round only exchanges public keys, which can be reused across sessions and have the same form as the keys Signal and WhatsApp already distribute. The apps’ handshake can serve as that first round, and our protocol adds one message to it.

  • X3DH with Deniable Authentication without Trusted Third Parties S. Jarecki, P. Nazarian, A. Rai. SCN 2026. ePrint
    Summary

    Our first design sends a proof that anyone can check. Such a proof works like a signature on the conversation, and deniability is lost. Here we replace it with a proof that only the intended recipient can check. Deniability is kept in every case but one, when a recipient has run out of one-time keys.

  • SAS-Model Authentication of Group Key Agreement without Extra Communication Flows S. Jarecki, A. Rai. CANS 2026, to appear.
    Summary

    Existing short-code schemes cost an extra round when applied to a group. We add the check to a two-round group key agreement protocol and to Sender Keys, the protocol behind group chats in Signal and WhatsApp, with no added rounds.

In preparation

  • Asymmetric Fuzzy PAKE for Remote Biometric Authentication with Protection of Server-held Biometric Data J. Xu, A. Rai, P. Nazarian, S. Jarecki, I. Harris.
    Summary

    The protocol works on the embeddings that a face-recognition network produces. It combines locality-sensitive hashing, under which two readings of one face mostly agree, with a password-style key exchange that tolerates a small set of errors. A closed-form model predicts false-acceptance rates far below what an experiment can measure.

  • Password-Authenticated Double-Key Agreement: Bootstrapping Anamorphic Communication from a Shared Password With S. Jarecki and J. Xu.
    Summary

    Anamorphic cryptography lets two people communicate covertly inside traffic that an authority mandates and can decrypt, provided they already share a secret key. We show how to establish that key from a shared password, inside the mandated traffic. The sessions must stay hidden after the two are forced to reveal the password. We prove that this takes at least three messages. Each of our three protocols uses exactly three and sends only strings that look uniformly random.

Working paper

  • What a Security Proof Cannot Prove: Types of Trust Assumptions and Their Grounds A. Rai. October 2026.
    Summary

    The paper sorts the assumptions behind a security proof by what each depends on, such as a party’s honesty or a secret staying secret, and by who must uphold it. What an assumption depends on decides which evidence can justify it. A trusted party’s honesty needs governance, such as audits and liability, while a password’s secrecy or the reliability of a human check can be measured. I applied the classification by hand to the web’s certificate system, to Signal and to synced passkeys.

Teaching

A single explanation rarely works for a whole room, so I keep several ready. One student wrote that when a problem was hard I tried “to introduce the topic to us in a different way.”
